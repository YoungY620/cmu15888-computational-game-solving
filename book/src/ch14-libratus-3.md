# L14 Libratus III: Self-Improver

The previous chapter covered Libratus's second module, the subgame solver, which re-solves the subgame that has actually been reached, in real time and in a finer-grained abstraction, using the blueprint strategy as context. This chapter completes the picture of Libratus: the two corrections that reach-maxmargin refinement needs when **many subgames are refined simultaneously**; the third module, the **self-improver**; and a design question that runs through the whole system — how Libratus turns human poker knowledge into abstraction.

## Solving Multiple Subgames Simultaneously

First recall reach-maxmargin refinement for a single subgame, from the previous chapter. In the coin-toss example, under the blueprint strategy P1 chooses Play with positive probability after being dealt Heads, even though Play has a lower expected value than Sell — so P1 is effectively "giving us a gift": if she enters the subgame holding Heads via Play, she is donating $0.5$ of expected value to us. In the augmented subgame we can therefore raise the alternative payoff of the Heads type by $0.5$ — even after the raise, Play is still a mistake for P1 there. The gadget-game solver no longer has to spend effort pushing down the EV of the Heads type and can **concentrate on reducing the EV of P1's other types for entering the subgame**. The amount by which the alternative payoff is raised is the **gift**, and it comes from mistakes the opponent has already made under the blueprint strategy.

In the real system, refinement does not solve one subgame at a time: once the river is reached, every public board corresponds to a subgame, and Libratus refines a large number of subgames at once. This breaks two premises of reach-maxmargin, and each requires a correction [Brown & Sandholm, AAAI-17 workshop, NeurIPS-17, *Science* 2018].

### Problem 1: off-path EVs no longer remain constant — split gifts by reach probability

When only one subgame is refined, the subgames that produce gifts keep playing the blueprint strategy, so their off-path EVs are constants and the size of each gift can be read directly from the blueprint. But **when many subgames are refined simultaneously, off-path EVs need not remain constant**: an improved strategy in one subgame changes the opponent's EV at the other (gift-producing) subgames, and hence changes the gifts themselves.

**Solution: split gifts among subgames by the probability each subgame is reached.**

![Refining multiple subgames simultaneously (subgames A and B highlighted). If P1 chooses Play after Heads she is gifting us value; her Sell alternative payoff is $0.5$. The chance node C below leads the game into subgame A or B with probability $0.5$ each, so the gift must be split between the two subgames by reach probability.](assets/ch14-libratus-3-multi1.png)

In the figure above, P1's Sell alternative payoff after Heads is $0.5$, so the total gift to be distributed is $0.5$; the chance node then leads into subgame A and subgame B with probability $P=0.5$ each. The slide animation demonstrates several legal allocations — for example $1.0$ to A and $0$ to B, or $0.5$ to each — as long as the reach-probability-weighted total does not exceed the gift:

$$0.5 \cdot g_A + 0.5 \cdot g_B \;\le\; 0.5 .$$

Each subgame's solver then "spends" only its own share of the gift. Intuitively, the safety argument for a single subgame requires that "the opponent's EV for entering this subgame is no higher than in the blueprint." After splitting gifts by reach probability, the gifts consumed by all refined subgames, weighted by reach probability and summed, still do not exceed the total gift; so along any path the opponent cannot obtain a higher EV than in the blueprint just because many refinements are happening at once — safety is preserved under simultaneous refinement.

### Problem 2: gifts themselves may be improved — substitute a lower bound

The second problem goes further: **a gift may not be as large as it appears, because the subgames that produce the gifts are themselves being refined**. In the figure, if P1's Sell subtree after Heads — the source of the alternative payoff — is also being refined, then the opponent's play there will improve and the "$0.5$ gift" shrinks. The slide animation shows this process: as the Sell subtree is improved, P1's EV there drops from $0.5$ to $0.25$, and the alternative payoffs at the Play branch, at subgame A, and at subgame B should all be lowered accordingly — if we still count them at the nominal value $0.5$, the alternative payoffs are overestimated and the safety argument fails.

![The gift-producing Sell subtree is highlighted: it is itself being improved, so the gift shrinks (in the animation its EV drops from $0.5$ to $0.25$). The solution is to substitute a lower bound on the gift.](assets/ch14-libratus-3-multi2.png)

**Solution: instead of the nominal value read from the blueprint, substitute a lower bound on the gift.** Then, even if the gift-producing subgame is improved to the point where the opponent makes no mistake there at all, the alternative payoffs in the augmented subgame are never overestimated, and safety is maintained.

Together, these two corrections allow Libratus to safely refine many subgames simultaneously on every round of every hand, while still exploiting the mistakes the opponent made in the current hand. Medium-scale experiments (refinement within the action abstraction) support this approach: reach-maxmargin refinement reduces the blueprint strategy's exploitability of $91.3$ mbb/hand (small game) and $41.4$ mbb/hand (large game) to $8.26$ and $5.50$ mbb/hand respectively — better than maxmargin refinement's $9.36$ / $6.12$, and far better than re-solve refinement's $81.2$ / $36.3$ (the full table and experimental setup appear in the previous chapter).

## The Self-Improver

### Why a third module is needed

The blueprint strategy's action abstraction is computed before the match and fixed: each decision point in the game tree permits only a handful of bet sizes. Human professionals deliberately probe with bet sizes outside the abstraction, testing the AI's blind spots. To the blueprint, these sizes are **holes** in the action tree: the blueprint has no corresponding action branch, and can only respond approximately through action translation — mapping the opponent's bet onto a nearby size in the abstraction — and such approximations can be attacked repeatedly. The job of the third module, the self-improver, is to discover and repair these holes automatically.

The blueprint strategy is computed offline before the match (one run used $1+195$ nodes and took 1–8 weeks), so recomputing the entire blueprint every night during the competition was impossible; the self-improver is the **only one of the three modules that modifies the blueprint during play**, and it therefore must take a "local patch" approach: freeze everything else and recompute only the small pieces of the game that contain holes. Its position in the architecture diagram makes the same point — its output is a "new action abstraction for part of the game," fed back into the blueprint module, rather than a brand-new blueprint strategy.

Note that the self-improver is **not** opponent modeling. The traditional use of machine learning in games is to build an opponent model, find systematic mistakes in the opponent's strategy (such as folding too often or calling too often), and exploit them; but exploiting the opponent also exposes oneself — the opponent can in turn exploit our deviations (the sole exception is the conservative family of "safe opponent exploitation" techniques [Ganzfried & Sandholm, TEAC-15 et al.]). So to a first approximation, Libratus does no opponent exploitation: it only borrows the opponents' actual betting data to **decide where to fill holes**, and once a hole is filled it still derives the strategy for the new branch by game-theoretic means (equilibrium computation). The resulting patches are universal — they hold against all opponents, not just the four players at hand.

![Libratus's three-module architecture (the self-improver highlighted). The self-improver feeds a "new action abstraction for part of the game" back into the blueprint module: it does not recompute the whole blueprint, but generates new action abstractions only for the parts the opponents exploit the most.](assets/ch14-libratus-3-arch.png)

### Filling holes in the action tree

The self-improver's core algorithm is **filling holes in the action tree**, in the following steps (see the figure):

1. **Locate**: find the top $k$ holes in the blueprint action abstraction that the opponents exploit the most — the betting points the opponents actually use but for which the blueprint has no branch;
2. **Freeze**: freeze the rest of the strategy — the existing blueprint strategy is left untouched, so the new computation cannot break the safety already achieved;
3. **Solve locally**: for each hole, compute an $\epsilon$-equilibrium of the partial game containing the new action. To concentrate the computation near the hole and keep the size manageable, asymmetric coarser abstractions are used on the two sides: Libratus's own subtrees use a coarser card abstraction, and the opponent's subtrees use a coarser card and action abstraction;
4. **Merge**: merge the newly computed action abstraction, together with its strategy, into the blueprint. From then on, when an opponent uses that bet size again, Libratus has a native response rather than a translated approximation.

The Science paper also records two forms of self-improvement. The first (more conservative): when computing an equilibrium for a hole, also include a **default sibling bet size** during equilibrium finding, so as not to assume that the opponent necessarily uses only the size that will be added. The second (more aggressive): no default sibling bet size — this is closer to "targeting" the opponent's actual habits, riskier and even somewhat exploitative; Libratus mitigates the risk by **enabling that part of the strategy during play only if the opponent indeed uses that bet size most of the time**.

![Filling holes in the action tree. Left box: freeze this part of the strategy; right box: compute an $\epsilon$-equilibrium for the part containing the hole — coarser card abstraction on Libratus's side, coarser card and action abstraction on the opponent's side. Repeat for the top $k$ holes.](assets/ch14-libratus-3-holes.png)

### How it ran during the competition

The 20 days of the Brains vs. AI rematch settled into a distinctive day-night rhythm: by day, the four professionals studied the Libratus hand histories they had received the previous evening, looking for and attacking its weaknesses; by night, Libratus's self-improver repaired its own most heavily exploited holes. The concrete hole-selection mechanism: each day, Libratus aggregated in aggregate the bet sizes its opponents used most heavily in the first betting round that day, and chose the top $k$ holes by **frequency of use** and **distance from the closest bet size in the abstraction**, for overnight response computation; the paper records that compute limitations dictated $k=3$, and the three holes could typically be computed to reasonable convergence accuracy within 24 hours. Each bet size that had reached reasonable convergence by morning was added to the blueprint strategy together with the newly computed strategy following it. The professionals widely reported that Libratus kept getting stronger — the weaknesses they found often stopped working within a few days. This capability is also one of the new features of the NeurIPS-17 subgame solver: "**changing the action abstraction between hands**" — Libratus did not play all 120,000 hands with one fixed abstraction.

In terms of scale, each self-improver run used 196–600 compute nodes on the Bridges supercomputer, typically lasting 8–30 hours — exactly covering one night.

## Turning Poker Knowledge into Abstraction

Finally, return to a fundamental design question about the first module: where does Libratus's "poker knowledge" come from? The answer: **almost entirely through the abstractions**. The equilibrium-finding algorithm (an improved version of MCCFR) and the subgame solver are domain-independent, general-purpose algorithms; what carries the poker-specific knowledge is the choice of abstractions. The paper adds two points about the improved MCCFR: during iterations it probabilistically skips actions whose regrets are very negative and clearly unpromising (about a 3× speedup, allowing larger abstractions to be solved); and this skipping incidentally mitigates the inherent flaw of imperfect-recall abstraction — decision points within a single bucket, whose optimal strategies differ, would otherwise "fight" to pull the bucket's shared strategy in different directions, and once decision points that will never be reached in actual play stop being updated, the decision points that do occur can move the bucket's strategy closer to their own optima.

**Abstracting chance's actions (the cards).** The same algorithm used in Tartanian8 [Brown, Ganzfried & Sandholm, AAMAS-15] — a distributed version of the state-of-the-art state-abstraction algorithm for centralized equilibrium finding presented in class: the computation is distributed across compute nodes (blades) based on the public flop cards, so that any one sample stays within a single node. The intuition is that similar boards are strategically nearly indistinguishable — a King-high flush and a Queen-high flush can be treated as the same hand (even though at the highest level of play exactly such distinctions decide winning and losing, which is why the last two rounds still need fine-grained treatment). But at much finer granularity:

- 1st and 2nd betting rounds: no card abstraction;
- 3rd betting round: 55 million card histories compressed into 2.5 million buckets;
- 4th betting round: 2.4 billion card histories compressed into 1.25 million buckets.

It must be emphasized that the card abstraction of the last two rounds exists only in the blueprint: **actual play never follows the blueprint's abstract solution in the last two rounds** — once the later rounds are reached, nested subgame solving solves in real time in a fine-grained abstraction with no card abstraction at all, so every concrete poker hand is considered individually during play. The predecessors of this algorithm family, Baby Tartanian8 and Tartanian7, won the 2016 and 2014 ACPC (Annual Computer Poker Competition) respectively.

**Abstracting the players' actions (bet sizes).** The betting space of no-limit hold'em is essentially continuous (any amount up to all-in) and must be discretized into finitely many actions. Libratus's set of bet sizes was largely based on observing **what top humans and top AIs actually do** — this guarantees the abstraction covers the sizes opponents are most likely to use; the paper further specifies that most of these sizes were **nice fractions or multiples of the pot**, roughly determined by analyzing the most common bet sizes taken by prior top AIs at various points in the game across past ACPCs. On top of this, radical bet sizes that are uncommon in the human arsenal were added, enabling the AI to mount attacks beyond the range of human habits; and some of the bet sizes early in the game tree were optimized with the method of [Brown & Sandholm, AAAI-14] — a domain-independent parameter optimization algorithm that converges to a locally optimal set of bet sizes.

In other words, human poker knowledge delineates "the set of candidate actions," and game theory decides "with what probability to use each of them." That Libratus plays a style humans find highly unusual — with radical bet sizes and mixed strategies — is precisely because the latter is driven entirely by equilibrium computation. The self-improver closes the loop: the candidate set itself is no longer fixed, and it keeps evolving during the competition — human knowledge provides the starting point, self-improvement provides the adaptation, and equilibrium computation provides the principled play.

## Experimental Evaluation of the Full System

The Science paper also reports a step-by-step ablation on full HUNL (average win rates with 95% confidence intervals), against the previously strongest HUNL AI, Baby Tartanian8, the 2016 ACPC winner (which had defeated all other competing AIs with statistical significance):

- Using only the **raw blueprint strategy**: Libratus **lost** to Baby Tartanian8 by $8 \pm 15$ mbb/hand;
- Adding state-of-the-art **post-processing** on the 3rd and 4th betting rounds (e.g., eliminating low-probability actions that are likely positive only owing to insufficient convergence): the blueprint instead won by $18 \pm 21$ mbb/hand. But post-processing makes the strategy more predictable and **increases exploitability**, so the full Libratus agent did not use post-processing on the last two rounds (on the first two rounds it used a new, more robust form of post-processing);
- Adding nested subgame solving (using only bet sizes within Baby Tartanian8's action abstraction): won by $59 \pm 28$ mbb/hand;
- Using the full nested subgame solving structure actually used in the competition: won by $63 \pm 28$ mbb/hand.

For comparison, Baby Tartanian8 beat the next two strongest AIs in the ACPC by only $12 \pm 10$ and $24 \pm 20$ mbb/hand — Libratus's advantage over it is order-of-magnitude. These numbers also quantify the contribution of the modules from the previous chapters and this chapter's opening: the blueprint alone is not enough to beat the strongest previous-generation AI; the subgame solver is what opens the gap.

The experiment against humans was the January 2017 Brains vs. AI rematch: 20 days, 120,000 hands, against four HUNL specialist professionals — Jason Les, Dong Kim, Daniel McCauley, and Jimmy Chou; of the \$200,000 prize pool each human was guaranteed \$20,000, with the remaining \$120,000 divided based on performance relative to the worst-performing of the four. Libratus won by an overall margin of $147$ mbb/hand, with $p = 0.0002$ if the hands are treated as independent and identically distributed (statistical significance $99.98\%$), and it **beat each of the four professionals individually**.

## Key Takeaways

- When many subgames are refined simultaneously, two premises of reach-maxmargin break. First, off-path EVs need not remain constant — split gifts among subgames by the probability each subgame is reached. Second, the gift-producing subgames are themselves being improved — substitute a lower bound on the gift rather than the nominal value. These two corrections preserve safety under simultaneous refinement.
- The self-improver works by **filling holes in the action tree**: locate the top $k$ holes the opponents exploit the most, freeze the rest of the strategy, compute an $\epsilon$-equilibrium of the local game containing the new action under asymmetric coarser abstractions on the two sides, then merge the new action abstraction into the blueprint. It does no opponent modeling/exploitation — it uses opponent data only to decide where to fill holes — so the patches are universal across all opponents.
- During the competition the self-improver ran every night ($k=3$, 196–600 nodes, 8–30 hours), letting Libratus change its action abstraction between hands; weaknesses found by the professionals stopped working within days. Self-improvement comes in two forms: a conservative patch with a default sibling bet size, and an aggressive patch enabled only when the opponent indeed uses that bet size most of the time.
- Libratus's poker knowledge enters the system almost entirely through the abstractions: the card abstraction reuses Tartanian8's distributed algorithm (the last two rounds compressed into 2.5M and 1.25M buckets, existing only in the blueprint — actual play uses no card abstraction in those rounds), and the action abstraction takes nice fractions/multiples of the pot based on the play of top humans and top ACPC AIs, plus radical bet sizes, plus optimized early-tree sizes; human knowledge sets the candidates, equilibrium computation sets their usage, and self-improvement keeps the candidate set evolving.
- Full-HUNL ablation: the raw blueprint loses to Baby Tartanian8 by $8 \pm 15$ mbb/hand, while adding nested subgame solving wins by $59$–$63 \pm 28$ mbb/hand; against four human specialists Libratus won by $147$ mbb/hand ($p=0.0002$), beating each one individually.
